I · Read this with the phone in reach

This is not a cure

Disclaimer

This book is one family's true story and general information. It is not veterinary advice. Parvo is an emergency. Only a licensed veterinarian can examine, diagnose, prescribe, or set a dose for your dog. If anything here disagrees with your veterinarian, follow your veterinarian. Blue and the Awesome Dudes are not responsible for any outcome.

This book will not cure a dog. Canine parvovirus has no kitchen recipe that puts water back into a vein. Left without care, most dogs who get sick with it die. With aggressive care, most live. The difference is not love versus medicine. The dogs in this yard who lived were loved, and they were on fluids.

If a dog is sick right now

Stop reading. Call a veterinarian or an emergency clinic. If the dog is vomiting, has diarrhea, is limp, or will not eat, this is an urgent veterinary assessment. Ask the veterinarian which fluid route is appropriate. Come back to this page after the dog has been seen.

Nothing in these pages is a dose. Nothing here is permission to start a catheter because a chapter made it sound simple. A dirty line, the wrong fluid, or the wrong rate can kill a dog the virus had not finished with. The account you are about to read is one yard, in one drought. It is not a protocol, and it is not a promise.

What the yard does teach is narrower, and harder to dodge. Eleven dogs, born April 13. Villager #1 tested positive on October 13. They were six months old. Three died while fluids were still late. The keeper believes that delay contributed. It is not a proven cause for each dog. Lily was put down. People played with the time, blamed a small yard, and said to put them down. The keeper knew they could survive. Eight did. In the keeper’s view, the eight were not allowed to get dehydrated. The whole effort cost about ten thousand dollars. It did not cost fifty-five thousand.

Hold those facts in that order. Not the yard. Not the drug. Not a fifty-five-thousand-dollar stay. The vein, before the dog dries out.

II · Before you do anything else

Stay safe

Signs of parvo: tiredness, not eating, vomiting, diarrhea (often bloody, with a strong smell), fever or a cold body, and a painful belly. Any puppy with these signs needs a veterinarian today.

Do not give

  • Human medicines: Pepto-Bismol, ibuprofen (Advil), naproxen (Aleve), acetaminophen (Tylenol), or aspirin. They can poison dogs or cause bleeding.
  • Imodium (loperamide): it can be dangerous, especially in collies, shepherds, and other herding breeds, and it can hide worsening.
  • Home cures: colloidal silver, garlic, ivermectin, essential oils, bleach drinks, or online parvo cures. None of them cure parvo, and some are poisons.
  • Don't force water or electrolyte drinks into a vomiting dog. It goes into the lungs or comes back up.

Puppies: keep them away from unknown dogs, dog parks, pet store floors, and rest stops until your vet says the vaccine series is complete.

Poison help: ASPCA Animal Poison Control, (888) 426-4435 (a fee may apply).

III · One keeper’s account

The yard

There were eleven dogs. They were born on April 13. Villager #1 tested positive on October 13. They were six months old, not six and a half.

Coyotes live around that place. It was a drought. The fields were dry enough to lift. When the farmers began to plow, dust kicked into the air and covered the dogs and the ground they lived on. After that, the dogs got sick with parvo. Dust is one possible way contaminated soil might have been moved. It is not a known source.

They had been digging holes for a couple of months. The keeper tried to make them stop. They had come from the same womb, and it showed. When one started a hole, the next one out continued it. Then the rest followed, and they dug the rest of it together. It was amazing. The keeper sees it now. If it was not the dust this year, it could have been from a different year, buried, and the digging brought it up.

The first night

Villager #1 played fine all day. That night he threw up all his food, the food that should have been processed during the day. He threw up in his crate. The next day he did not want to move. He ended up with watery poop. The day after that they took him to the vet, thinking he had a blockage. The vet ran a parvo test. It came back positive.

Within a few days Lily and Villager #2 were sick. They refused to eat. They could not hold down any water. By the end of four days every dog was sick the same way. They could not hold water. They stood in the yard like drooling zombies, their eyes sagging from dehydration.

The fluids had to be prescribed

The fluids have to be prescribed by a veterinarian. For a few days they were unattainable. That is why the keeper could not save them all. A person cannot invent the bag. It has to be prescribed, and it has to arrive before the eyes sag.

IV · What you are actually fighting

What the virus does

Canine parvovirus is a small, hard virus. The strains in dogs today belong to the family called CPV-2, including the variants written 2a, 2b, and 2c. The vaccines in common use are made to cover those variants. This is not the human illness called fifth disease. People do not catch parvo from their dogs. People do carry it from dog to dog on their shoes.

It prefers cells that divide quickly. In a puppy or an unprotected dog, that means the lining of the intestine and the bone marrow. The gut stops absorbing and starts to slough. Vomiting and diarrhea follow, often with blood, often with a smell a keeper does not forget. The marrow slows its work on white cells, so bacteria that belong in the gut cross into the blood. The dog feels poisoned because, in a plain sense, the dog is.

Very young puppies can take the virus into the heart. That form is less common than the gut form. It is not the story this yard told. The story this yard told was thirst that was not answered.

How it moves

The time from exposure to illness is often three to seven days. A dog can shed virus in stool before anyone sees a sick face. The amount of stool required to infect the next dog is small. Once the virus is in soil, bedding, or a crack in a floor, it can stay infectious for months, and sometimes longer than a year. Cold weather does not reliably kill it. Most household disinfectants do not either.

A veterinarian confirms it with a fecal antigen test, and sometimes with a PCR. A test can miss an early case. A modified-live vaccine given in the days just before can, occasionally, make a test faintly positive. Tests are for the person who is about to treat. They are not a reason to watch a limp dog overnight with an empty vein.

The odds, said carefully

Without treatment, clinical parvo is often fatal. With appropriate supportive care, the Merck Veterinary Manual puts survival around 70 to 90 percent. Those are populations, not promises. A dog can be treated properly and still die. A dog can look nearly gone and walk out. This yard ended at three dead and eight alive. That count is true. It is not a rate you can take to another kennel and trust.

V · Why the three did not come back

The fluids were late

Parvo empties a dog. Fluid leaves in vomit. Fluid leaves in diarrhea. The dog will not drink, or drinks and gives it back. Potassium can fall. Sugar can fall, especially in the young, who have little stored. The feet get cold. The gums go from moist to tacky to pale. Then the dog is too tired to stand. Dehydration can kill. So can a bloodstream infection, and so can a crash in blood sugar or electrolytes. Looking at a dog cannot sort those causes apart.

That sequence can take a day. In a small puppy it can take a few hours. A careful clinic will still name the virus as the disease. This book cannot name the thing that stopped each heart. Lily was put down. The others died while fluids were still away. The keeper believes the delay contributed. That is a belief, not an autopsy.

VI · Drought, plows, and what dust can carry

After the fields were turned

The illness showed itself after the plowing started. The keeper’s memory is specific. Drought. Coyotes living around the place. Farmers turning dry fields. Dust kicked into the air, onto the dogs, onto everything. The dogs were six months old.

Parvovirus is not a germ that dirt invents when the weather is bad, and a small yard does not invent it either. Dogs shed it in feces. Other dogs can leave it where a yard can meet it. It sits in soil because it is hard to kill. A plow in a drought can lift that soil and throw dust across a yard. Dust is a possible way contaminated soil might have been moved. It is not a known source, and breathing dust is not the route veterinary sources describe. Dogs take the virus in by mouth, from feces and from contaminated ground, shoes, bowls, and other dogs.

People blamed the small yard and said to put the dogs down. The yard made it easier for the virus to reach all eleven once it was there. It was not a reason they had to die. The keeper knew they could survive if they were not allowed to dehydrate. Eight did.

Do not wait for a field to be turned before you vaccinate. Do not blame a plow, or a coyote, or the size of a yard, and then skip a veterinarian. The order the keeper remembers is dust, then sick dogs. That order is a memory. No one can prove the plow dust carried the virus. Parvo spreads when a dog takes in virus shed in feces, and when it meets contaminated soil, shoes, and other dogs. That is what to guard against.

VII · The page to remember

Fluids first

A dog with parvo dies of what it loses. Replace what it loses before the body is empty, and you have done the thing that changes the odds. In this yard that was the only factor the keeper credits. Every drug in the later chapters was extra. None of them replaced the water.

A hospital puts that water in a vein: a catheter and a measured drip. That is the right door for a dog who is already flat, cold, or no longer absorbing fluid from under the skin. The dogs who recovered at home got a different door, and the keeper is exact about it. The fluid went under the skin at the scruff of the neck, where the skin is loose. You lift that skin and make a tent. The fluid has to go into the pocket of the tent, the subcutaneous space, so the dog can absorb it. It must not go into the muscle. Muscle is the wrong place. It is not a pocket, it does not absorb the way the space under the skin does, and it is not what saved these dogs.

Lift the scruff. Make a tent. The fluid goes in that pocket, under the skin, so the dog can absorb it. Not into the muscle.
Do not invent the amount

Too little, too late, and the dog dies dry. Too much, or another full dose while the last one is still sitting under the skin, and you have traded dehydration for a fluid imbalance. The amount is what a veterinarian writes for that dog. Do not invent one.

What goes in the bag

The fluid is a balanced isotonic crystalloid. Clinics hang lactated Ringer’s solution, Normosol-R, or Plasma-Lyte. People call these IV fluids because that is how the bag is labeled. Under the skin, it is the same bag, given into the tent. It is not sterile water. It is not salt water from a kitchen. Do not add sugar or potassium to the bag that is going under the skin. Those corrections, when a dog needs them, belong in a vein, where someone can watch the heart.

Start, then stay with it

The keeper’s dogs weighed about sixty pounds. One veterinarian wrote about 500 milliliters, twice a day, with anti-nausea medicine, for these dogs, into the tent at the scruff, until they felt better. That is what one veterinarian wrote for these dogs. It is not a dose for yours, and it is not a route for yours. The route depends on the dog in front of the veterinarian. Fluid under the skin may be chosen when dehydration is milder. A vein is preferred when dehydration is severe, or when the dog cannot keep water down. Published outpatient care is still a veterinarian’s plan, and the dog is still checked. You do not wait to see if they will die first. Before they just die, you get them seen.

Feeling better is not a single wag while the gums are still sticky. It is a dog who is up, whose gums stay wet between the doses the veterinarian scheduled, who is no longer pouring out more than you are putting in. Until then, you do what that veterinarian wrote. Before they die, and until they feel better.

Before the evening dose, look at the morning pocket. If it is still sitting there, the dog has not absorbed it. Give less, or skip it, and call the veterinarian who wrote the plan. A body that is cold does not pull fluid out of the skin. If the pocket never leaves and the dog is getting colder or going limp, stop. That dog needs a vein.

In the vein, the clock is slower than people think

A dog who is cold, flat, or past about ten percent dry does not get the day’s fluid under the skin. A hospital puts a catheter in a vein. In the 2024 AAHA fluid example of a parvo puppy, the deficit was replaced over about twelve hours, and a puppy’s maintenance was figured near 100 milliliters per kilogram per day, added on top of that rate. After the deficit was in, the rate came down to maintenance, if the puppy was not still pouring fluid out in vomit and diarrhea. Ongoing losses get added. They do not get ignored, and they do not get corrected by opening the roller clamp all the way.

A shock bolus in a vein is a veterinarian’s act, given in pieces, with a hand on the gums and the chest between each piece. This book will not print a home instruction for emptying a bag into a vein. Fluid that fast, in the wrong dog, floods the lungs. That is the imbalance on the other side of dehydration. Coughing, open-mouth breathing, sudden restlessness, or swelling after a large dose means stop and get to a clinic. No urine for many hours, gums going sticky again, and the dog’s own skin staying tented means the amount was late or short. Do not wait out of politeness.

The tent is still the place

The milliliters mean nothing if they go into the muscle. Lift the scruff. Make the tent. The pocket under the skin absorbs the dose. A cold, limp dog does not get another pocket. That dog gets a vein.

Anti-nausea medicine keeps the dog from pouring the water back out. Medicines in this class include maropitant and ondansetron. This book will not give you a milligram. Ask for nausea control the same day the fluids start.

My dog is vomiting. I think it is parvo. Do not let this dog get dehydrated. Start fluids now. We can talk about the other drugs after the water is in.

If the answer is a wait, ask who can see the dog sooner, and go there. Ask that veterinarian which fluid route is appropriate. The monoclonal antibody can be discussed once care has started. The antibiotic can be discussed once care has started. The estimate can be printed once care has started. The three in this yard did not get that order. The keeper believes the delay in fluids contributed. That is not a finding that names the medical cause of each death. Lily was put down.

What fluids do not do

They do not kill the virus. They keep the dog alive while the gut lining grows back, which takes days. During those days the pocket has to be filled again before it runs dry, or a vein has to stay open, and a person has to be watching. Sugar and potassium can still fall. That is why a clinic checks them. A bag left in an empty room is not care.

VIII · One bill, over five thousand dollars

Three days in the hospital

Villager #1 caught parvo first. He was in the hospital for three days. The treatment included the new monoclonal antibody. That stay was five thousand dollars. The whole yard, eight dogs saved, cost about ten thousand dollars. It did not cost fifty-five thousand.

Read the number honestly. In many emergency hospitals, three days of parvo care — a catheter, fluids around the clock, anti-nausea medicine, nursing, cleaning, tests — already costs thousands before a newer drug is added. Five thousand dollars for that stay is painful, and in a lot of counties it is believable. It is one dog’s bill. It is not a national price. A different clinic, a smaller dog, or a shorter stay can be less. A collapsed puppy in a city emergency room can be more.

Ask what the fluids, the nausea medicine, and the nursing cost before you accept a single number that folds in everything. You are allowed to ask for the line tonight and the decision about a three-day admission in the next sentence. Asking does not make you a worse owner.

What those three days are for

A vein is kept open. Fluids are matched to how dry the dog is, then to what the dog is still losing. Potassium and glucose are watched, because both fall in this disease, and either one can stop a dog who “has fluids running” if nobody looks. Pain is treated. The hind end is cleaned so the skin does not burn. Food comes back in small amounts once the vomiting eases. Starving a parvo dog for days is an old habit. The gut heals better with a little work to do, when the dog can hold food down.

Some dogs need plasma if their blood protein collapses. Some need more than three days. This dog was there for three. That is the fact from the yard. It is not a script.

The expensive stay is not the moral opposite of the dogs at home. The hospital dog had a vein and a person awake all night. The dogs at home had a tent of skin at the scruff and someone who would not let that pocket run dry. Both are stories about dehydration. The drug is the next chapter. It is not the reason either dog got water.

IX · The new drug, named plainly

The monoclonal antibody

The drug in that hospital stay is a canine parvovirus monoclonal antibody. Elanco developed it. Clinics have known it as CPMA, and it has also gone under the name Trutect. A veterinarian gives it. It is not a bottle that arrives on a porch.

For treatment it is one dose, into a vein. The labeled use is for dogs eight weeks of age and older. The antibody is built to bind the virus and keep it from entering cells. That is passive immunity: borrowed defense, fast, and temporary. It does not teach the dog’s immune system the way a vaccine does. It is not a vaccine. A vaccine is not a cheaper version of it.

In a controlled challenge study, puppies were infected, tested positive, and on that same day given either the antibody or a placebo, with no other treatment. None of the treated dogs in that study died. Dogs on the placebo did. The treated dogs also stopped vomiting and returned to eating sooner. The company has reported high survival — figures around ninety-three percent — and shorter hospital stays among the patients its clinics tracked. Work in shelters has found shorter stays when the antibody is added to ordinary nursing.

Hold the study the way it was run

Those challenge dogs were treated the day they first tested positive, not after two days of bloody vomit and no fluids. Company numbers are the company’s. An antibody does not grow a new intestine. It does not pull a dog back from dehydration by itself. A dog can receive this drug and still die the way the three died, if the vein stays empty.

If a clinic has it, ask two questions. Will you start fluids in this hour? And what does the antibody cost by itself, added to a fluid plan you can actually pay? Earlier is better, because the point of the drug is to limit further damage. Earlier does not mean waiting for a vial to thaw before anyone places a catheter. If the vial is at another hospital, the fluids do not wait for the drive.

Veterinarians also have a preventive use of this antibody for dogs who were exposed and are not yet sick. That is a label question and a clinic question. It is not something to invent from a field book.

X · One receipt, $555

The smaller bill

Brutus was brought back to clinic 2, the same place that had just seen Mrs Sassy Fancy Boots. They waited three hours before he was taken. He was given fluids and a shot. The bill was five hundred fifty-five dollars.

That is a different building from a three-day ward, and a different kind of bill. An exam, a test, an antibiotic shot, fluids, and something for nausea can land near that number in plenty of practices. It can also land somewhere else. Treat $555 as this yard’s receipt, not as a price you were promised.

The antibiotic needs a clean sentence. Parvovirus is a virus. Antibiotics do not kill it. They are there because a damaged gut lets bacteria into the blood, and a dog with few white cells cannot fight them off. One dog in this yard got that shot. The dogs who recovered at home did not. This book will not tell you the antibiotic saved the eleven, and it will not tell you that skipping it is a clever economy. A veterinarian looks at how sick the dog is and whether the dog is staying. You watch them decide — and you still insist the fluids are already running.

The smaller bill is also proof that “doing something” is not always the five-thousand-dollar door. Ask for the smaller plan by name: fluids, nausea control, and whatever else they believe this dog needs today. Then ask whether the dog is too sick for it. A dog who is cold, collapsed, or still a very young puppy is a poor candidate for going home on hope. Honest outpatient plans are rechecks, not one shot and a disappearance.

XI · What the rest actually recovered on

Love, rest, and fluids

The rest of the dogs recovered on fluids given under the skin, and on anti-nausea medicine. No extra treatments. The keeper’s words for everything around that are love, rest, and not letting them dehydrate.

The tent at the scruff

The place is the loose skin at the back of the neck, the scruff. You lift it until it makes a tent. That tent is a pocket of subcutaneous space, the space under the skin and above the muscle. The fluid is introduced into that pocket. The dog absorbs it from there. If the fluid goes into the muscle instead, it is in the wrong tissue. It is not sitting where it can be taken up the way a subcutaneous pocket is taken up. The keeper is plain about this. The pocket. Not the muscle.

You can see it when it is right. The skin rises in a soft lump as the pocket fills, and over the next hours the lump eases as the dog absorbs it. You do it again before the dog is dry, not after. Anti-nausea medicine is what keeps vomiting from emptying them faster than the pocket can fill them. Love is the person who stays and does it again. Rest is a quiet room. Those are the treatments. They are not a stack of extra drugs.

Do not sweeten that into a different story. They are not saying the dogs healed because they were cherished instead of treated. And they are not saying a bag of fluid stabbed at random into a neck is the same thing as a tent. The eight lived because they were not allowed to get dehydrated, and the fluid that prevented it was in that pocket.

That is the lesson the yard is allowed to teach. The monoclonal antibody was one dog’s care. The antibiotic was one dog’s care. The group at home did not get a secret third product. They got the part of parvo medicine that has existed for decades: replace the water, stop the vomiting, give the gut days, and do not leave the dog alone in it.

This chapter is not a how-to

A wrong fluid, a dirty needle, or fluid pushed into muscle instead of the pocket can hurt a dog parvo had not finished with. Have a veterinarian show you the tent the first time. Use the amount and schedule your veterinarian wrote, and give less or nothing if the last pocket is still sitting there. If the dog is cold, limp, or the lump is not absorbing, stop and get fluid into a vein.

The turn that means drive

  • Ears or feet going cold.
  • Gums pale, gray, or sticky.
  • A dog who cannot stand.
  • Vomiting that will not stop after anti-nausea medicine.
  • A belly that hurts to the touch.
  • More blood than before, or no urine.

That is not a night for love to out-argue the body. That is a drive back to a building with a cage and a person awake.

Nursing that does help, once a veterinarian’s plan is already in motion: warmth without cooking the dog, water available but not forced down a throat that is still throwing up, tiny meals after the nausea is controlled, and a floor you can bleach. Pouring a large volume of an oral electrolyte into a vomiting dog wastes the hour and delays the vein. The mouth is the wrong door when the stomach will not keep the water.

XII · The vaccination, put in its place

The shot is not the fluids

The keeper gave a parvo vaccination as soon as the first dog was known to be sick. That was the right kind of hurry for the dogs who were not yet down. It is the wrong story about the dogs who were.

A modified-live parvo vaccine can protect a susceptible dog in about three to five days. During outbreaks, shelters vaccinate healthy dogs immediately for that reason. The shot races the incubation period. In a dog whose mother’s antibodies are no longer in the way, one dose often starts protection. Puppies are harder. Maternal antibody blocks the vaccine for weeks, which is why puppies need a series: usually beginning around six to eight weeks, then every two to four weeks until at least sixteen weeks, and often out to eighteen or twenty weeks where the risk is high. An adult already on schedule is a different animal from a puppy on the first shot of three.

A vaccine does not put water under the skin or into a vein. It does not stop diarrhea that has already started. It does not repair bone marrow. It is not what the keeper credits for the dogs who recovered. Those dogs were not allowed to dehydrate. At home, that meant fluid in a tented pocket at the scruff, and medicine so they would stop vomiting it away.

Keep both facts, in this order

Vaccinate the dogs who are still well, the day you know parvo is in the yard. Do it because three to five days is a real window for a healthy dog, and the virus does not wait. Then go to the sick ones and talk about a veterinarian, not about the syringe you just used on their kennel-mates.

Do not let anyone sell you the shot as a cure they can give instead of a line. Do not let this chapter talk you out of vaccines in a quiet year. The series, given before the plow and before the dust, is how a yard stays boring. Boring is the goal.

XIII · What you can clean, and what you cannot

The ground

You cannot bleach a field. You can stop tracking the field through the house.

Parvovirus lives in stool. Pick up every stool and bag it. Keep the sick dogs on a surface you can actually wash. Dirt, gravel, and cracked wood hold the virus. Sun and time lower the amount outdoors. They do not make a freshly contaminated yard safe for a new puppy. Months is a reasonable fear. Longer than a year is not a paranoid one. A hole dug this year can open what a sick dog left in another year. The dogs in this yard had been digging for a couple of months, one starting a hole and the next one out finishing it, and then the rest following. The dust of the drought may have carried it. The ground they opened may have been holding it already. Ask a veterinarian before a half-vaccinated pup is allowed onto that ground.

On a hard surface, clean the filth off first. Bleach does not work through feces. Then use ordinary household bleach, the kind that is about five to six percent sodium hypochlorite, diluted one part bleach to thirty-two parts water. Keep the surface wet for about ten minutes. Accelerated hydrogen peroxide products whose labels name parvovirus are the other common choice, and some of them tolerate a little dirt better than bleach does. Do not bet a puppy on a “kennel cleaner” that never says parvovirus on the bottle.

Separate shoes, bowls, bedding, and the person who just held the sick dog. Wash hands. Change clothes before you touch a dog who is still well. You are not protecting yourself from a human disease. You are protecting the next dog from your boots. The vaccine and the bleach do the same kind of job: they shrink how much virus reaches a dog who is not already fighting it. Neither one rehydrates the dog who is.

XIV · Nothing on this page is for sale

For the three

Villager #2, Lily, and Mrs Sassy Fancy Boots are not in the yard.

Villager #1 stayed three days. The keeper asked how long before the rest would get sick. Lily became sick while he was still in the hospital. Then Villager #2. They were told the fluids were ready. The drive was an hour and a half. The fluids were not there. Lily and Villager #2 were rushed to the hospital after that. Villager #2 died on the way. Lily was the runt, and she was put down after they were told she would probably not recover and would probably have a low quality of life. That is how the first two died. Mrs Sassy Fancy Boots rode to clinic 2 with Brutus. The ride was rough. She was worse, so she went in. They had left at 12:30. The rental counter held them two hours, and the dealership took another thirty minutes with a car whose transmission was going bad. They arrived at 5:30 and were put on emergency time, fifteen minutes past regular hours. She received only fluids, about two hundred and fifty dollars, because the emergency prices were exaggerated. Brutus was brought back to that same clinic for fluids and a shot. On the way home they stopped at clinic 1 for the fluids that had not been there two days before. By the time they got home, Sassy was dead. A three-hour wait on top of a three-hour round trip. Time was the enemy. Nobody close takes a new patient or an emergency. When Brutus broke his leg, the closest place was Bradley, and the specialist was in Savoy. That was about a thousand dollars, and a couple of hundred in gas.

Eight lived. Two of the eight who lived, Villager #1 and Brutus, were treated at a clinic. The other six recovered at the house, on fluids and anti-nausea medicine, and nothing else. In the keeper’s view, the eight were not allowed to dehydrate. Survival is not proof that the cheapest path was right for the sickest dog. Death is not proof that the keeper failed to love them. People said the yard was too small and the dogs should be put down. The keeper knew they could survive. Eight did. The keeper believes three were lost while time was wasted. Parvo can also cause bloodstream infection and dangerous changes in blood sugar and electrolytes. This account cannot assign one cause to each death. Both the count and the uncertainty are true, and neither one is a lie you get to tell a person burying a dog.

If you are reading this with a dog in the car, you already know which chapter matters. Get the dog seen. Ask which fluid route is appropriate. The rest of the book will wait. If you are reading this after, and the dog is gone, the wait is the thing to be angry at. Not the fact that you are not a hospital. Not the dust, by itself. Not a shot you did or did not give. The clock. The keeper believes three were lost while time was spent on something other than getting fluids. That belief is not a cause of death for each dog.

This page is for Villager #2, for Lily, and for Mrs Sassy Fancy Boots.

XV · A list to hold at the counter

Take this page in

Check a line if it helps you remember it. Nothing below is a dose. If the person who can see your dog contradicts a line, believe the person. They can hear the heart. A list cannot.

  1. Say it before you sit down: this dog needs to be seen now. Ask the veterinarian which fluid route is right. We can talk about other drugs after that.
  2. Ask for anti-nausea medicine in the same visit, not the next morning. Vomiting pours the fluid back out.
  3. Ask the price of fluids, nausea control, and nursing by themselves, before one number that folds in a three-day stay.
  4. If the monoclonal antibody is in the building, it can be added after the line is in. If it is not, do not wait on a delivery before fluids.
  5. Let the veterinarian decide about an antibiotic. It does not kill the virus, and it is not a reason to delay the drip.
  6. If a veterinarian sends fluids home, they go in a tent of skin at the scruff, in the amount and schedule the vet writes for your dog. Ask the vet to show you how. Ask how often to come back for a recheck.
  7. Cold, limp, pale or gray gums, a painful belly, vomiting that will not stop: that is not a night to stay home.
  8. Vaccinate the dogs who are still well, today. Only vaccinate dogs that are healthy. Never give a vaccine to a dog that is already sick. Ask your vet first. Do not let anyone sell the shot as a cure for the dog who is already down.
  9. Separate the sick dogs. Pick up stool. Bleach what you can actually clean. Keep new puppies off that ground.

XVI · What is memory, and what is medicine

Where this came from

Eight of the eleven lived. Villager #1 and Brutus were treated at clinics and lived. The other six of those eight were kept at the house on fluids and anti-nausea medicine. Lily, Villager #2, and Mrs Sassy Fancy Boots were taken for care too. The next fluids did not get back in time.

The odds in this yard

Eight lived. Three died. Eleven dogs. Eight of eleven is the count. It is not a promise for the next yard, and it is not a smaller number than it was. Anyone who says the dogs were not taken care of is not describing what happened here.

For anyone who asks

Villager #1 stayed three days at clinic 1 and lived. Brutus was brought back to clinic 2, waited three hours, and lived after fluids and a shot. Lily got sick while Villager #1 was still admitted. Two days after clinic 1 had no fluids, she and Villager #2 were rushed to the hospital. Villager #2 died on the way. Lily was put down. Mrs Sassy Fancy Boots was seen first at clinic 2, only fluids, about two hundred and fifty dollars, because a 5:30 arrival was billed as emergency time. She was dead when they got home with Brutus, after the wait, the round trip, and the stop back at clinic 1.

Eight of eleven lived. Sassy had already received fluids. She died on the floor because the way back took too long.

The bills are earlier in the book. Villager #1’s stay at clinic 1 was five thousand dollars. Mrs Sassy Fancy Boots’s emergency visit at clinic 2 was about two hundred and fifty dollars, fluids only. Brutus’s return to that clinic was five hundred fifty-five dollars, fluids and a shot, after three hours of waiting. She died at home before the next fluids arrived. The whole effort cost about ten thousand dollars. It did not cost fifty-five thousand. The dogs who got the next dose in time got better.

The rest of this chapter is the keeper’s account set next to ordinary published medicine. The count above is the part to hand to a neighbor. The dogs were cared for. Eight of them are the proof.
